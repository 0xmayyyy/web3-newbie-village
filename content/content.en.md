## What is a blockchain?

**A blockchain is a public ledger that many people keep together, and that is hard for one person to quietly change.**

You can think of a blockchain as a record system maintained by more than one party.
Transactions are confirmed by the rules and written into the record. Participants agree on the record through a consensus mechanism.

One important property is that it resists tampering.
When the network is running normally, and its consensus and security assumptions hold, a record that is already well confirmed cannot be changed on a whim by one person.

A record that resists tampering does not mean the information that was entered, a project's marketing, or the other side of a trade is true.

**A cryptocurrency is a digital asset on a chain; there are many kinds, and their risks differ.**

Cryptocurrencies are digital assets managed with cryptography and the rules of a network.

## Where BTC came from

**Bitcoin was the first cryptocurrency; it started running in 2009, and its supply is capped at about 21 million coins.**

October 31, 2008
An author using the name "Satoshi Nakamoto" published the Bitcoin white paper :
《Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System》
The usual Chinese translation is "Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System"

The question in the white paper is direct: can two people pay each other online without relying on a financial institution to stop the same money from being spent twice?
It combines digital signatures, proof of work, and network consensus, and designs a peer-to-peer electronic cash system.

On January 3, 2009, the Bitcoin genesis block was created, and the Bitcoin network started running.
Satoshi Nakamoto's real identity has never been confirmed in a way people agree on.

Under the current consensus rules, the issuance cap for BTC is about 21 million coins.
It is not issued by a company whenever the company wants more. It is produced gradually under the network's rules.
Scarcity is one of its properties. It does not guarantee that the price only rises.

## What is a stablecoin?

**A stablecoin aims to stay 1:1 with the dollar, but it is not a bank deposit, and it can lose that peg.**

A stablecoin aims to track a reference value, often 1 US dollar, for example USDT and USDC.
Different stablecoins may use fiat reserves, crypto collateral, or other mechanisms.

A stablecoin can be used for pricing, transfers, and settling trades.
It is not a bank deposit, and it is not guaranteed to stay redeemable at 1 dollar.
You need to understand the reserves, the redemption terms, freeze permissions, and the risk of losing the peg.

# 02｜Primary and secondary markets 🌱

## Primary market: issuance and early funding

**The primary market is the subscription when a coin is first issued, and early does not mean easier money.**

The primary market usually means the first issuance, the funding, or the subscription.
In crypto, common forms include private sales, public sales, token issuances, and some Launchpad events.
The exact category depends on what the participant is buying, who the seller is, and how the deal is structured.

Fictional teaching example: a project opens a token subscription to participants for the first time, and the participants receive the tokens from the issuer.
That is usually a primary issuance.
It is not a real project, and it does not mean taking part this way will make money.

Early participation can mean a longer lockup, less public information, and a harder exit.
"Early" only means earlier in time. It does not mean easier money.

## Secondary market: trading coins already issued

**The secondary market is buying and selling coins that someone else already holds.**

The secondary market usually means assets that have already been issued, moving between holders.
Buying BTC that someone else is selling on an exchange, or swapping an already issued token on a DEX, is generally a secondary trade.

So primary / secondary describes issuance and later circulation.
CEX / DEX describes the venue and the mechanism.
They are not the same classification.

## What are lockups and unlocks?

**A lockup means you cannot sell for a while, and after the unlock, more of the coin can be sold.**

A lockup means the asset cannot be transferred or withdrawn for a time, under certain conditions.
An unlock means the restriction is lifted on a schedule.
An early subscription may be delivered in batches. Finishing the subscription does not mean you can sell immediately.

An unlock changes the supply that can circulate.
The actual effect on price still depends on what holders do, on demand, and on market liquidity.
An unlock does not mean the price will definitely rise.

## Trading pairs

BTC/USDT prices the BTC on the left in the USDT on the right; you can buy a fraction, and a sale usually pays you the quote asset.

You see "BTC/USDT: 60,000" on an exchange and wonder whether you must buy a whole BTC.

You do not.
**BTC/USDT means the BTC is priced in USDT.**
The BTC on the left is the asset you buy or sell, and the USDT on the right is the quote asset.
Fictional teaching example: 60,000 means that, at this quote, 1 BTC is about 60,000 USDT.

Fictional teaching example: suppose you only want 0.01 BTC.
60,000 × 0.01 = **600 USDT**, before fees.
A sale reverses the direction: you sell BTC and usually receive USDT.
Exchanges often allow a fractional amount, but they set a minimum order size or value.

**Easy to mix up:** BTC/USDT and BTC/USD are not necessarily the same market;
USDT is a stablecoin, not dollars in a bank account.
Before you trade, look at the quote asset on the right.

Coin A sells for 0.01 dollars, and coin B sells for 100 dollars.
Which one is cheaper?
**The unit price alone cannot tell you.**

Start with market cap.
A common formula is: **circulating market cap = current price × circulating supply.**

Fictional teaching example: coin A has 10 billion coins in circulation.
0.01 × 10 billion = **100 million dollars**.
Coin B has 100,000 coins in circulation.
100 × 100,000 = **10 million dollars**.
A has the lower unit price, and its circulating market cap is ten times B's.

Then look at **FDV (fully diluted valuation)**: it usually multiplies the current price by total supply or max supply, and the data site decides the exact definition.
Fictional teaching example: suppose coin A now has 10 billion circulating, and the future total supply might reach 100 billion. At 0.01 dollars, the FDV is about **1 billion dollars**.
That is a valuation measure. **It does not mean 1 billion dollars actually flowed into the project.**

**Easy to mix up:** market cap is not "the total cash every holder put in";
FDV is not a price or a size the project is guaranteed to reach.

## Supply

Separate circulating supply, total supply, and max supply; an unlock changes whether coins can be sold, and issuance is what adds new coins to supply.

Imagine a cake cut into 100 slices. You hold 1 slice, which is 1%.
The project rules later add another 100 slices, and you still hold 1, so your share becomes 0.5%.

Tokens also change in quantity, and three ideas have to stay separate:

- **Circulating supply:** by a data site's definition, the amount currently counted as circulating in the market.
- **Total supply:** usually the issued amount after some burned amount is removed, and the definition can differ.
- **Max supply:** if the protocol sets a cap, that is the most that can be issued;
some coins have no fixed cap.

Also separate **issuance** and **unlock**.
Issuance puts new tokens into the supply;
an unlock usually removes a transfer restriction from tokens that were already allocated or issued.
Both can change how much can be sold, and they are not the same event.

**What you need to check is not simply "how many coins exist." Who can issue more? Whose coins unlock, and when? Who might they sell to after the unlock?**

## Liquidity and volume

Volume is how much already traded, and liquidity is whether a large order now would punch through the price.

You want to sell 50,000 dollars of a coin, and the page says "24-hour volume 100 million dollars," which sounds busy.
You open the book and find very few people willing to take your sell order right now.

**Volume is how much traded over a past period; liquidity is whether you can trade now without moving the price much.**
The two numbers are related, and they are not the same thing.

Fictional teaching example: a coin traded 100 million dollars over the last 24 hours, but only 2,000 dollars of bids sit near the best bid.
A market sell of 50,000 dollars may walk down into cheaper bids, and the average fill can be far from the price on the screen.

Some pairs can also show wash trading or unreliable volume statistics.
A large volume does not mean the book is deep enough.

## Spread, price impact, and slippage

The spread, price impact, and slippage are three related but different things, and the price on the screen is not necessarily your fill.

On the trade page, the best bid might be 0.99 dollars and the best ask 1.00 dollars.
The 0.01 dollar gap is the **bid-ask spread**.

If you want to buy a lot, the cheapest ask is not enough, so you keep buying the next, more expensive level.
That change in fill price, caused by order size and book depth, is **price impact**.

**Slippage** is the gap between the price you expected and the price you actually got. It can come from the market moving, thin liquidity, and other causes.
The three are related, and they are not one word.

Fictional teaching example: the ask book has two levels, 100 coins at 1.00 dollars and 100 coins at 1.05 dollars.
A market buy of 150 coins costs 100 × 1.00 + 50 × 1.05 = **152.50 dollars**, about **1.0167 dollars** on average, not 1 dollar for every coin.

## Trading fees and network fees

The price can stay still while fees, the spread, slippage, a withdrawal fee, and network fees still eat the balance a little at a time.

What beginners most often miss is not the rise or the fall. It is the cost of each step.

Buying can include a payment-channel quote gap and a trading fee;
trading on an exchange can include the spread and slippage;
withdrawing from an exchange can include a withdrawal fee;
an on-chain approval or swap can also create a network fee or a protocol fee.

Note: **an exchange withdrawal fee is not necessarily the on-chain network fee**.
An internal transfer on the platform is usually not an on-chain transfer, and the fee structure is different.

## How do profit and loss work?

After a drop, the percentage rise needed to get back is larger than the drop, because the base of the calculation is already smaller.

Fictional teaching example: you buy a coin with 100 USDT, it falls 50%, and 50 USDT is left.
If it then rises 50%, that is only 50 × 1.5 = **75 USDT**, still 25 USDT short.

That is because **the base of the percentage changed**.
To get from 50 back to 100, it has to rise 50 ÷ 50 = **100%**.

The simplest spot profit and loss, before fees:

**Profit before fees = proceeds of the sale − cost of the buy.**

**Return before fees = profit ÷ cost of the buy × 100%.**

Fictional teaching example: you put in 100 USDT and sell for 110 USDT. Before fees you made 10 USDT, a 10% return.
What you actually keep still has to subtract trading fees, withdrawal fees, and the rest.
Buys and sells in several lots, a different quote asset, or a contract position need a separate calculation.

## Before you trade, you should be able to answer

Before you press buy, say what you are buying, what it costs in total, whether you can get out, and whether a total loss would affect your life.

You do not need a dozen indicators before you are allowed to buy a first coin.
Before you press "buy," you should be able to say four things:

**First, what am I buying?**
Is it BTC, a stablecoin, or a token from a project you do not know?
What is it quoted in?

**Second, what do I actually pay?**
Besides the coin price, are there fees, a spread, slippage, a withdrawal fee, or a network fee?

**Third, can I get out?**
Is there a lockup, an unlock, a platform withdrawal limit, thin liquidity, or a contract-permission risk?

**Fourth, what is the worst case?**
Can this money be lost entirely?
If it is, would that affect your life?

If you cannot answer one of them, look it up first. You do not have to force an order to chase the market.

## What are deposits and withdrawals?

A deposit puts money onto an exchange; selling a coin is not fiat in the bank, and an on-chain withdrawal and a bank withdrawal are two different paths.

Your bank account has 100 units of fiat, and you want a first BTC.
Paying that fiat through a compliant channel, and ending with an asset in the exchange account, is what people usually call a **deposit**.
Later you sell the coin and, through a channel your location supports, withdraw the fiat back to the bank. That completes a **fiat withdrawal**.

"Withdrawal" can also mean **withdrawing the coin**: sending the exchange's USDT to your own wallet.
That is not the same thing as withdrawing to a bank.

Keep three places in mind:

- **Bank account:** holds fiat, handled by the bank and the payment system.
- **Exchange account:** the platform records your balance;
the platform holds the matching assets.
- **Self-custody wallet:** you control the on-chain assets with a key. A wrong transfer or a leaked key often cannot be undone.

Teaching simulation: you sell BTC on the exchange and receive 100 USDT.
What you hold is still USDT, **not 100 dollars that have already arrived in the bank**.
You still need a fiat conversion and withdrawal channel your location allows, and you check the rate, the fees, and the limits.

**Easy to miss:** "the exchange says the withdrawal succeeded" is not "the bank has credited it";
"the coin has been sold" is not "the fiat has arrived."

## Step 2: sign up and lock the account down

Lock the official entrance, the email, the login, withdrawals, and recovery before you put money into a new account.

Depositing the moment you finish registering is like moving valuables into a new house before the keys are stored.

Follow this order:

1. **Confirm the official entrance:** enter through a reliable official channel, and check the full domain, the app developer, and the platform prompt;
an ad ranking is not an official certification.
2. **Protect the email:** email is an important way back into the account. Use a separate strong password, and turn on the two-factor option the email supports.
3. **Protect the exchange login:** set a strong password that is different from the email. Prefer a passkey or authenticator the platform supports, and store the recovery method safely.
4. **Set withdrawal protection:** turn on an address allowlist, an anti-phishing code, device management, and unusual-activity alerts when the platform offers them.
5. **Complete any required KYC:** submit identity documents only in an official flow you have confirmed. Do not send an ID to "support" that messaged you first.

## P2P, in particular

In P2P a screenshot is not a credit; check the real bank credit and the payer inside the order, then follow the platform's rules.

A common P2P case is you trading with another user while the platform provides the order and an escrow mechanism under its rules.
**Escrow does not mean a bank transfer has no risk.**

If you are the one selling coins and the other person sends a "transfer succeeded" screenshot, do not release the coins just because you are in a hurry.
A screenshot can be forged, a text can be faked, and some credits can still be disputed later.

A safer order of steps:

1. Talk only inside the platform order, and check who you are trading with and what the payment requires.
2. Use an account in your own name, as the platform rules require;
stay alert to a third-party payment or a name that does not match.
3. Log in to the bank's official channel yourself, and check the real available balance, the transfer details, and the payer.
4. Only after that matches the order rules do you confirm the release, through the platform's process.
5. If there is a dispute, stop, keep the order, the chat, and the bank record, and appeal on the platform.

**Easy to miss:** "release first, I will pay in a moment," or a request to move the chat off the platform, is a reason to pause.
Do not read a freeze, a chargeback, or fraud-related funds as "if the balance shows the money, it is absolutely safe."

## Order types

A market order fills soon but does not fix the price, and a limit order fixes the price but may not fill.

## Position size and a risk budget

Decide the most this trade may lose, then work out how much to buy.

First decide "if this judgment is wrong, how much loss am I prepared to take," then work out how much to buy.
The risk amount here is not the amount you invest, and it is not the margin.

Suppose the account has **10,000 USDT (often written 10,000 U)**, and the planned risk for this trade is 1%.
The calculation has three steps:

**A study rule for risk: set the planned risk of one trade at 1% of the account; a more aggressive cap is 2%.** This is not a safety standard that fits everyone. If you are unsure, use a lower share or do not trade.

Suppose the account has **10,000 USDT (often written 10,000 U)**, and the planned risk for this trade is 1%.
The calculation has three steps:

**Step 1: how much are you allowed to lose?**
10,000 × 1% = **100 USDT**.
That is the loss you hope to keep this trade near, if the planned stop is hit.

**Step 2: if you buy a whole BTC, how much do you lose down to the stop?**
The plan is to buy at 60,000 USDT and stop at 59,000 USDT.
The gap per BTC is 60,000 − 59,000 = **1,000 USDT**.
So 1 BTC, bought and sold at those two prices, loses 1,000 USDT;
0.1 BTC loses 100 USDT.

**Step 3: so how much should you buy?**
You are allowed to lose 100 USDT, and each coin might lose 1,000 USDT.
100 ÷ 1,000 = **0.1 BTC**.
At entry, the notional value of this position is 0.1 × 60,000 = **6,000 USDT**.

**Formula: position size = (account funds × planned risk share for one trade) ÷ |entry price − stop price|.**
The result is a quantity of the asset, for example a number of BTC;
multiply by the entry price to get the notional amount.
The absolute value keeps the gap positive for both a buy and a short.
This fits a simplified spot or linear-contract calculation. An inverse contract and similar products need their own rules.

**What does leverage change here?**
For the same 0.1 BTC position, in a simplified case, 2x needs about 3,000 USDT of initial margin, and 10x needs about 600 USDT.
The position size and the buy and sell prices are the same, so the profit and loss from the price move is the same. Using only 600 USDT of margin does not make this loss smaller.

Higher leverage also brings liquidation closer.
The planned risk above holds only if the position stays the same, it is not liquidated early, and the stop fills as expected.
Fees, funding, slippage, and a failed execution also change the real loss.
Leave room in the budget for costs, and check where liquidation sits. Do not treat "100 U" as a guaranteed ceiling.

## Three traps that show up constantly in crypto

**Dirty-money freezes, pig-butchering scams, and letting someone use your account to collect funds are three of the most common traps.**

Do not take a payment from a third party, and do not collect money for someone else.

That can be using your account to move money from a bad source.

In a serious case it can also be concealing or hiding criminal proceeds.

# 11｜How to research a project 🔍

## Question 1: what does it do?

**If you cannot say what the project does in one sentence, do not buy it yet.**

Say, in one sentence, the problem it works on, who it is for, and what the product is. If all you can repeat is "revolutionary" and "empowering the ecosystem", and you cannot say how anyone uses it, keep researching.

## Question 2: what is the coin for?

**A good project is not the same as a useful coin. Ask what the coin actually does.**

A good product does not mean the token captures value. Check what the token is for, how supply and demand work, what rights holders actually have, where fees go, and who governs.

"Platform revenue is growing" does not automatically mean that revenue is paid to token holders. A buyback plan also has to be checked: is it actually carried out, and can it continue?

## Question 3: who holds the coins, and when can they sell?

**See who holds the coins and when they unlock, so you are not the one left under a dump.**

Look at allocations to the team, investors, the community, and the treasury, the share already circulating, and the unlock schedule. Concentration of holdings has to be read with who the address is: an exchange's pooled address is not necessarily one whale.

## Extra check: which exchanges already list it?

**Whether it is listed is decided by the exchange's own announcement.**

Check the exchange's official asset page and announcements. Do not stop at the project's poster. Check the contract, the network, the pair, and whether trading, deposits, and withdrawals are actually open.

Separate a spot listing, a contract listing, pre-market trading, and a token that some wallet's DEX aggregator happens to show. A token searchable in a brand's wallet is not the same as that brand's CEX having officially listed the spot market. Also check for recent delistings, pauses, or restriction notices.

## If it is new: who led the round, and which institutions are in?

**An institutional investment can be checked. It does not mean the price will rise.**

Look up the round, the date, the amount that was made public, the lead, and the other investors. Cross-check announcements from the project and from the institution itself. Equity investment, a token investment, a grant, a technical partnership, and simply showing up at an event are not the same relationship.

"Institutional backing" is best written as a fact you can verify, for example "this institution joined this round". Do not treat logos on a website as proof of a partnership. Do not treat an investor's name as a promise about the future price.

## Question 4: what do the numbers say?

**Before you trust a number, ask how it was counted. A marketing screenshot needs its original source.**

TVL is the value of locked assets under a specific set of rules. The coin price and double counting can move it.
Active addresses are not the same as distinct real people.
Volume is not revenue.
Protocol fees, protocol revenue, and net profit are not the same metric.

Before you compare, check the date, the unit, the time range, and the definition. For a screenshot in a pitch, try to find the original source.

## Question 5: what powers exist, and how do you get out?

**Check who can mint, freeze, or change the rules before you consider buying.**

Who can upgrade the contract, pause it, mint more, freeze, or change fees? Can you sell normally? Is there a transfer tax, a blacklist, or a transfer restriction? Who controls the pool's liquidity?

A safety scanner can only offer some clues. It cannot guarantee there is no malicious logic. A small test buy and sell also cannot prove you will be able to exit later.

## What does an audit mean?

**An audit means someone reviewed a specific version of the code. It is not a promise of no bugs.**

An audit inspects specific code and a specific scope. It may find problems. It is not proof that there are no bugs. Read what was audited, the version, the date, the issues still open, and whether the code that shipped matches the version that was audited.

## Sources, and who is being paid to talk

**Official docs and on-chain data come first. KOLs come after, and notice whether they are paid.**

Read official docs, on-chain records, and independent data before community chatter. Fundraising, listings, celebrity attention, and partnership announcements all need checking. None of them is a promise of profit.

When you read a KOL, notice whether they hold the asset, whether they are paid to promote it, or whether they use a referral link. Those interests change what they emphasize.

## DeFi: financial apps on a chain

**DeFi is banking and exchange functions on a chain. There is no support desk to catch you.**

DeFi means apps that use smart contracts for swaps, lending, and similar financial functions. You usually interact with a wallet. Costs include network fees and protocol fees. Risk can come from the code, the collateral, oracles, governance powers, and the economic design.

## Lending

**Lending is about collateral and liquidation. Do not look only at the yield.**

Depositing an asset may earn a return. Borrowing an asset requires collateral and interest. If the collateral price falls, you can be liquidated. The deposit side also has bad-debt, liquidity, and contract risk. Do not look only at the yield.

## Liquidity pools

**Providing liquidity earns fees, and you also take impermanent loss.**

You put assets into a pool and may earn trading fees or rewards. Besides the assets' own price moves, there is impermanent loss, rewards that lose value, and contract risk. Impermanent loss describes the value difference versus simply holding the assets. It is not guaranteed to "come back".

## Staking and yield products

**If a product is called "earn", first ask what the money is actually doing.**

Native staking on a proof-of-stake network supports validation and pays rewards. There can be an exit wait, validator risk, or a penalty mechanism.

A product a platform labels "staking" or "Earn" may actually be lending or some other yield strategy. You have to read what the funds are used for, the redemption rules, and who bears the risk. The product name is not enough.

APR is an annualized rate without compounding. APY includes a compounding assumption. An annualized number on the screen is not a guaranteed return for the next year.

## Airdrops, points, and testnets

**Airdrops and points do not guarantee a payoff. Do not approve things blindly for them.**

You may get a chance at a future distribution by using a product or joining an activity. The cost includes time, network fees, and interaction risk. Points do not guarantee a token redemption. Test assets usually have no mainnet value, and there may be no reward.

## NFTs

**Buying an NFT does not mean you bought the copyright to the picture.**

An NFT is a token used to represent a unique identifier and related information. Buying an NFT does not automatically give you copyright in an image or other legal rights. The actual rights depend on the license and the terms. Price, liquidity, metadata, and whether the project continues are all risks.

## On-chain games and DAOs

**A game lasts if it is fun to play. Holding a governance token is not the same as being a shareholder.**

An on-chain game puts some game assets or mechanics on a chain. Whether the game is fun, and whether the token economics can last, are separate questions.
A DAO is a way of organizing people with rules and a governance mechanism. Holding a governance token does not mean you own equity in a company. Governance power can also be highly concentrated.

## Meme coins

**A meme coin can surge, and it can go to zero. Only use money you can afford to lose.**

Meme coins often grow around a culture, a community, and how the story spreads. They can rise by astonishing amounts, and a few participants make a lot. They can also go to zero quickly, or become impossible to sell, and participants lose most or all of the principal.

People often compare buying a meme coin to "buying a lottery ticket", because the outcome is highly uncertain and it is easy to see only the get-rich stories. It does not have a lottery's fixed, transparent prize mechanism. Concentrated holdings, insider advantage, bots front-running, malicious contracts, and liquidity also change the result.

So you can understand the upside, and you also have to hold the chance of going to zero, of being unable to sell, and of uneven information in the same picture. Do not treat a get-rich story as the expected result for an ordinary participant.

## What is a prediction market? 🔮

**A prediction market is trading on the outcome of a question.**

For example:  
"Will this team win?"  
"Will this product ship before the end of the year?"

You can buy "yes" or "no". If you are right, you are paid under the platform's rules. If you are wrong, the share you bought can go to zero.

Believing the event will happen does not mean buying in is guaranteed to make money. The price you pay, the fees, and the rules that decide the outcome all change the result.

Before you join, read:  
when it closes, what counts as "it happened", and who decides.

## RWAs and tokenized stocks

**A tokenized stock is not the same as you actually holding the stock.**

RWA means an on-chain representation of a claim related to a real-world asset. A tokenized stock gives some exposure to a stock's price, or some related right. It is not the same as being the registered holder of the stock.

Check the issuing entity, custody, the legal right, redemption, dividends, who is allowed to participate, and trading hours. A name that looks like a stock does not mean the rights are the same.

## DePIN and AI

**For DePIN and AI projects, first look for real demand and real revenue.**

DePIN usually means using a network and incentives to organize real-world infrastructure, such as compute, communications, or sensors. An AI and Web3 project may involve compute, data, or automated agents.

Check real demand, hardware cost, where revenue comes from, and how permissions are set. If an AI agent can operate a wallet, be explicit about how much it may spend, what it can call, and how you revoke that permission.

Note: For every activity, ask four things: what it is, how you take part, what it costs, and how you can lose.

A success on-chain and a credit on the platform are two different things. Arrival is not guaranteed.

# 16｜Common questions and practice ✅

## Do I have to buy a coin before I can learn Web3?

**You can learn without buying.**

No. You can read, watch the market, use a paper trade, or use a test network. Learning and investing are two different things.

## Is USDT the same as the US dollar?

**USDT is not the dollar. It tries to stay near 1:1 with the dollar.**

No. It is a stablecoin that aims to track the dollar's value. Issuance, redemption, reserves, and risks are not the same as dollar cash or a bank deposit.

## BTC is too expensive. What if I cannot buy a whole one?

**You can buy 0.001. You do not have to buy a whole coin.**

You can usually buy a fraction. You do not have to buy one whole coin. You still have to meet the platform's minimum order, and you still have to understand the asset's risk.

## If it is in a cold wallet, can it never be stolen?

**A cold wallet can still be stolen. The usual causes are the seed phrase and signing the wrong thing.**

There is no guarantee. A leaked seed phrase, a device problem, a malicious signature, and a mistaken action can all cause a loss.

## Is an exchange wallet the same as that brand's Web3 wallet?

**Same brand, and the exchange account and the on-chain wallet are still two different things.**

Not necessarily. The same brand does not mean the same custody. Read, for the specific product, who controls the keys, how recovery works, and how withdrawals work.

## Is an airdrop always free?

**An airdrop is not always free. A "claim it free" page can also be a scam.**

Not always. It can cost time, network fees, or even funds, and there may be no reward at the end. A "claim it free" page can also be a scam.

## If a project was audited and listed, is it safe?

**An audit and a listing are not a safety guarantee.**

They can be part of the research. They do not replace a full judgment of risk, and they do not guarantee a return or that the assets are safe.

## If I learn the indicators, can I make money steadily?

**Learning the indicators does not mean steady profits.**

You cannot infer that. Indicators have to be read against the market and against how you execute. Fees, risk management, and emotion also change the result.

## Does crypto have no weekends?

**Crypto trades 24 hours. You do not have to watch it for 24 hours.**

Many crypto markets trade continuously. Bank deposits, some assets, and platform maintenance can still have hours. The market being open does not mean a person has to stare at it.

## Safety quiz

1. "Official support" asks for your seed phrase to repair the wallet. Do you give it?
Answer: No. Stop the conversation and check the channel.

2. An Ethereum address and a Base address can look the same. Can you pick the network at random?
Answer: No. The recipient has to support that network and that asset.

3. If you disconnect a site, are previous token approvals revoked?
Answer: Usually not. You have to check them separately.

4. A coin priced at 0.001 dollar is always cheaper than a coin priced at 100 dollars. True?
Answer: You cannot judge it that way. You also have to look at supply, valuation, and the asset itself.

5. You set a stop. Is the real loss guaranteed to stay within the amount you planned?
Answer: No. Slippage, a price jump, how the order works, and execution problems can all change the result.

6. A stablecoin shows 20% annualized. Does that mean you are guaranteed 20% more after one year?
Answer: No. The annualized figure is an estimate under current conditions. It changes with the market. It is not a guaranteed return.
